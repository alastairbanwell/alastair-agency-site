Trust

Why I Won’t Put Fake Numbers On Your Website.

A client sent me a design with invented like counts and a match result that never happened. Padding the numbers feels harmless. Here is what it actually costs you.

October 2026

A client sent me a reference design last week for a section he wanted on his site. It was a wall of social media posts, and it looked great. Photos, captions, a video thumbnail. Underneath each one, the numbers: 325 likes, 12 comments, 68 reactions. One post announced a 2-1 lead in the second half.

None of it was real. The photos were AI-generated, the captions were invented, the match had never been played and the numbers had been typed in by whoever made the mockup.

He was not trying to deceive anyone. He had seen a layout he liked and sent it over, which is exactly what a client should do. But building it as drawn would have meant hard-coding a fictional scoreline onto a real organisation's website, and that is where I had to say no.

Why padding the numbers is so tempting

Every small business hits this. You are new, or you are quiet, or you had a good year three years ago and a flat one since. The honest version of your website looks thin. Four reviews. A couple of hundred followers. One case study.

Meanwhile every competitor's site seems to be bursting with activity. So you round up. You use the busy month instead of the average one. You write "trusted by hundreds of families" because it feels true even though nobody counted. You add a testimonial from someone who definitely would have said it if you had asked.

It feels harmless, because the intent is not to con anybody. It is to stop looking like the newest, smallest option on the page.

The first problem: it is checkable

This is the part people underestimate. Your website claims the Instagram account is thriving. Your Instagram account is two clicks away and has 180 followers and a post from April.

Nobody checks every claim. But the ones who do check are exactly the people you want. They are the ones who are seriously considering spending money with you, which is why they are looking closely in the first place. The casual browser never notices. The buyer does.

And once they find one thing that does not match, they stop believing the rest of the page. Your actual qualifications, your real reviews, your genuine experience, all of it gets re-read as possibly inflated too. You contaminate your true claims with your padded ones.

The second problem: it freezes

Fake numbers are typed in. Typed-in numbers never change.

That post saying "2 hours ago" will still say "2 hours ago" next March. The 325 likes will be 325 likes forever. A visitor who comes back twice sees the same frozen moment, and frozen is a much louder signal than small. It tells them nothing has happened here in a long time, which is the precise opposite of what the section was for.

Real numbers have a quality fake ones cannot imitate: they move.

The third problem, and the one that actually costs you

If your website says you are busier than you are, you stop fixing the reason you are not busy.

A site showing four honest reviews makes the gap obvious. It nags at you. It makes "ask three clients for a review this month" an obvious job. A site showing a fabricated wall of activity makes that job disappear, because the problem looks solved from the outside.

I see this in website briefs constantly. People want to build the appearance of the business they want rather than the business they have. The appearance is quicker and it photographs well. It also means a year later you are in exactly the same position, with a nicer-looking website.

What to do when you genuinely have very little

There is a real answer here, and it is not "just be honest and accept a worse website."

Use specifics instead of scale. "Over 30 qualified coaches across three locations" beats "trusted by the community" even though it is a smaller claim, because it is concrete and it could be checked. Specificity reads as confidence. Vagueness reads as hiding something, even when you are not.

Show the work rather than the reaction. You may not have 500 likes on a training video. You do have the training video. Photographs of real sessions, real faces, real equipment and real places do the trust job that engagement counts were only ever a proxy for.

Four real reviews beat forty invented ones. Name, context, and something specific enough that it obviously came out of a person's mouth. One review saying "my daughter was too nervous to join in for the first three weeks and now she asks to go early" is worth more than a page of five-star generics.

Leave the gap visible rather than filling it with fiction. On that same project we removed two sections that said "Coming Soon" because empty promises made the whole page look unfinished. We did not replace them with invented content. We took them out and the page got stronger. An honest, smaller site beats a padded, bigger one.

What I told the client

We built the section. Real club photos, real channels, real links, proper design. It looks close to the reference he sent.

What it does not have is a single invented number or a match result that never happened. If the board wants genuinely live posts with real engagement, that is possible, it just costs money every month and needs connecting up properly. That is a real decision with a real trade-off, and it was his to make with the price in front of him.

What was not on the table was typing in numbers and hoping nobody checks.

The thing underneath all of this

Small businesses win on trust. It is the one advantage you have that larger competitors cannot buy, because you are the person who answers the phone and shows up and remembers the customer's name.

Fake social proof spends that advantage to look slightly bigger. It is a bad trade, and it is a bad trade even when it works, because the version of you that people trust is the real one.
