What Actually Makes A Small Business Website Convert.

Not theory, lessons pulled directly from building live sites for a carpenter, a landscaper, and a sports brand.

September 2026

I've built and launched three real, live sites this year: a carpentry and fencing business, a landscaping company, and my own sports brand before that. None of them had a marketing budget. None of them needed one. What actually moved the needle had almost nothing to do with clever design, and everything to do with a handful of boring, unglamorous basics that most small business sites still get wrong.

A real phone number, not a form

Every one of these sites leads with a phone number and a WhatsApp button, not a contact form. A homeowner deciding whether to call a carpenter or a landscaper wants to talk to a person immediately, not fill in a form and wait for a callback that might not come. If your business runs on trust and urgency, get out of the way and let people call.

Real photos of real work, every time

Stock photography is the fastest way to make a local business site feel fake. Every "before and after" and every gallery photo on these sites is of actual work, for actual clients, on that actual coastline. It's slower to put together than grabbing stock images, but it's the difference between a site that feels like a template and one that feels like proof.

Answer the actual questions people ask before they call

Every FAQ section on these sites came from real questions, not guessed ones: do you provide free quotes, do you work alone or with a team, how long does a job usually take. Skipping a question because you don't have a polished answer yet is worse than just asking the business owner directly and publishing the honest version.

Page speed is a trust signal, not just a technical metric

Uncompressed camera photos and unedited screen recordings will quietly wreck a site's load time without ever looking "wrong" in a screenshot. On one of these builds, the images folder alone went from 88MB to 30MB with zero visible quality loss, just by compressing everything properly before launch. Someone deciding whether to call a tradesperson on their phone, on mobile data, isn't going to wait for a slow page to prove itself.

One clear next step, repeated everywhere

Every page ends the same way on all three sites: a direct call to action, and a lower-commitment fallback for anyone not ready yet. No page should ever end with just a footer and no clear next step.

None of this required a big budget or a clever gimmick. It required treating the basics as non-negotiable instead of decoration.

If your details currently sit on a club page, a directory or a marketplace rather than a site of your own, that difference matters more than it looks.
